What is holiday pay in Ireland?

Holiday pay in Ireland means the pay you receive while you are on paid annual leave. Holiday pay entitlement Ireland is governed by section 19 of the Organisation of Working Time Act 1997. It is not a bonus and it is not extra money on top of your wages: it is your normal pay, paid for weeks when you are not at work. Every employee under a contract of employment earns it from the day they start work Holiday pay explained in one line: your normal pay, paid while you take your statutory leave., whether they are full-time, part-time, casual, agency or apprentice staff. There is no waiting period and no job where the statutory entitlement is zero.

Annual leave vs holiday pay

Annual leave is the time off; holiday pay is the money for that time off. The two are often confused, but the distinction matters: your entitlement is first worked out as time (weeks and days of leave), and only then turned into money using your normal weekly rate. Public holidays are a third, separate thing Ireland holiday pay law is national: the same Act applies in Dublin and Donegal alike.: the ten public holidays in Ireland sit outside your four weeks of annual leave and are not deducted from it. If a public holiday falls during your annual leave, that day counts as a public holiday, not as annual leave.

How to calculate holiday pay in Ireland: the three methods

How to calculate holiday pay in Ireland is a question with three answers, and the law says you get the best one. Method A – the 1,365-hour rule: 4 working weeks in a leave year in which you worked at least 1,365 hours. This method does not apply in a leave year in which you changed employment.

Method B – the 117-hour month rule: one third of a working week for each month in the leave year in which you worked at least 117 hours. Twelve such months give four weeks.

Method C – the 8% rule: 8% of the hours you worked in the leave year, subject to a maximum of four working weeks. To turn the hours into weeks, divide by your normal weekly hours.

Worked examples: how much holiday pay am I entitled to?

Example 1 – full-time worker

You worked 1,800 hours in the leave year, had 12 months with at least 117 hours, work five days a week at eight hours a day, and earn €650 a week. Method A: 1,800 is more than 1,365, so 4 weeks, which is 20 days. Method B: 12 ÷ 3 = 4 weeks, also 20 days. Method C: 8% × 1,800 = 144 hours, and your normal week is 40 hours, so 144 ÷ 40 = 3.6 weeks, or 18 days. The best method is four weeks, 20 days, and the holiday pay value is 4 × €650 = €2,600.

Example 2 – part-time worker

How to calculate holiday pay for part time workers is where the three methods really matter. You worked 800 hours, had 10 months with at least 117 hours, work three days a week at six hours a day, and earn €300 a week. Method A does not apply, because 800 is below 1,365. Method B: 10 ÷ 3 = 3.33 weeks, times three days = 10 days. Method C: 8% × 800 = 64 hours, and your normal week is 18 hours, so 64 ÷ 18 = 3.56 weeks, or 10.67 days. The best method is 3.56 weeks, 10.67 days, and the holiday pay value is 3.56 × €300 = €1,066.67. Note that a working week for you is three days, so four weeks would only ever be 12 days, not 20.

Example 3 – short employment

You worked for eight weeks and did 200 hours, on a five-day week at eight hours a day, earning €400 a week. Method C gives 8% × 200 = 16 hours of leave, which is 0.4 weeks or 2 days. Method B, with two months at 117 hours, gives 2 ÷ 3 = 0.67 weeks, or 3.33 days. Method A does not apply. Your entitlement is 3.33 days, worth 0.67 × €400 = €266.67. Even a short job earns leave from day one.

Example 4 – public holiday pay for a part-time worker

You work three days a week and earn €300 a week, and you worked 45 hours in the five weeks before a public holiday. You qualify, because 45 is more than 40. If the holiday falls on a day you normally work, a day's pay is €300 ÷ 3 = €100. If it falls on a day you do not normally work, you get one fifth of your weekly wage: €300 ÷ 5 = €60. Overtime and holiday pay

Overtime and holiday pay interact in two different ways, and they are often mixed up. First, hours: all hours worked count towards the hours used in the methods, and that includes overtime hours, because method C uses 8% of the hours you worked in the leave year. Overtime hours therefore build up your leave entitlement. Second, pay: holiday pay is paid at your normal weekly rate. If your pay varies from week to week, for example because of commission, bonuses or variable hours, your holiday pay is the average of your pay over the 13 weeks before you take the leave. Whether regular overtime counts inside that 13-week average depends on your contract and on whether the overtime is a regular, guaranteed part of your pay, so if overtime is a large part of your income, ask payroll how your average is calculated. An overtime and holiday pay calculator can only be exact once that question is answered, which is why this calculator takes your normal weekly pay as an input rather than guessing it.

Holiday pay per hour and holiday pay per month are just two ways of expressing the same entitlement: divide your weekly pay by your normal weekly hours for an hourly figure, or multiply it by 4.33 for a rough monthly figure. The statutory calculation itself always works in weeks, which is why the calculator asks for weekly pay.
